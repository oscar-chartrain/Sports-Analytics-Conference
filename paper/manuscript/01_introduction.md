# 1. Introduction

Ask a commentator what separates Carlos Alcaraz from Jannik Sinner and you will often hear a version of the same story. Sinner is metronomic: deep, heavy, hard to read only because it is so hard to hit through. Alcaraz is the improviser, the drop shot at 30–40, the shot nobody expected; and when the match is on the line, the story goes, flair wins the big points. The claim combines two ideas that are each measurable. One is about style: some players are more predictable in what they hit than others. The other is about pressure: some players raise their level on the points that matter, and others do not.

Both halves are now measured routinely. Leverage-based clutch metrics, which weight each point by how much it changes the probability of winning the match, are published by Tennis Abstract [ref], were presented by Stats Perform at this conference [ref], and are quoted in coverage of individual players. Shot-by-shot charting of professional matches makes it possible to measure style just as precisely. Yet the link between them has never been tested, and, more basically, nobody has checked whether a player's clutch score is stable enough to describe the player at all.

This paper does both, in that order. We measure shot-selection consistency as conditional entropy, how predictable a player's reply is given the ball they receive, and clutch performance as a leverage-weighted score in the Morris [ref] tradition, using 1.1 million charted points from the 91 most-charted men and, in an independently pre-registered replication, [WTA_N_POINTS] points from the 55 most-charted women. Our findings are:

1. **Clutch scores are much less reliable than they look.** Measured twice, on two independent halves of each player's matches, entropy agrees with itself almost perfectly (reliability 0.90–0.93). Clutch scores do not: reliability is 0.57–0.58 on serve, below the conventional 0.70 threshold, and 0.23 (ATP) and 0.06 (WTA) on return. Tennis Abstract's Balanced Leverage Ratio, the published metric, reaches only [BLR_ATP] (ATP). Three alternative constructions fail to fix the problem; statistical shrinkage helps but does not close the gap.
2. **What it would take.** At current reliability, a return clutch score needs roughly [K_RET]× more data than the median player in our pool has, about [M_RET] charted matches, to become trustworthy, and individual rankings move by [RANK_W] places from one plausible sample to the next.
3. **Predictability does not predict clutch.** Conditional entropy shows no relationship with clutch performance on either tour, in either role, under any of five leverage specifications, or in a pooled two-tour test. Equivalence tests bound the observed ATP correlation below 0.21 on both serve and return. Correcting for measurement error loosens these bounds (to 0.29 on serve and 0.41 on return) but leaves them informative; for WTA return, the correction leaves no bound at all, which is itself a consequence of the first finding.

The first finding is the more consequential. Scouts, broadcasters and bettors use clutch numbers to tell players apart; a metric with reliability near 0.2 mostly tells apart the matches that happened to be charted. We end with concrete rules for when a tennis clutch number can be quoted, and a reliability check that applies equally to clutch statistics in any sport. All code is public at [GitHub link].
